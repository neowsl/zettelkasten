We can compare minerals to cells in the human body. Cells (organic chemistry) are mostly built from carbon, oxygen, and hydrogen. On the other hand, minerals are built primarily from silicon and oxygen.

Two main ways to classify minerals:
1. Chemical composition
2. Atomic arrangement

Three secondary ways:
1. Must be solid with a crystal/lattice structure
2. Must be inorganic - e.g. clam shells don't count
3. Naturally occurring - e.g. minerals made in labs don't count

Two ways of formation:
1. Crystallisation from magma melt
    - **Inclusion:** Inside crystal must be older than outside crystal
2. Precipitation out of an aqueous solution

**Only 7** crystal forms that define basically all the minerals of the world.

- The number of protons in the nucleus defines the chemical properties of the element (e.g. oxygen will always have 8 protons in its nucleus).
- The number of protons plus neutrons within the nucleus of an atom defines its mass. Electrons have negligible mass.
    - Generally, the more difference there is between # of protons/neutrons, the higher chance of being radioactive.
- The electrons are arranged around the nucleus of the atom in orbits (or energy levels).
    - 1st orbit holds 2 electrons, 2nd 8, 3rd 8

**Charge balance:** # of protons = # of electrons

Whoa... So atoms want to fill their orbitals with electrons to capacity. A "vacant" spot in an orbital is called a **valency**. So in order to be stable, atoms can *give up or take* electrons to form **bonds**.

E.g. lithium has 3 protons, so 2 fit in the first orbital, but only 1 goes in the second. Because of entropy, lithium finds it "easier" to *give up* that extra electron.

All elements down a column have the same "needs" - e.g. because sodium is in the same column as lithium (and it has 11 protons), we fill 2 + 8 + 1 orbitals, and it wants to give up that last one.

***THIS IS SO COOL!!!***

Noble gasses don't need to give up or take any electrons, so they don't bond. They can have electrostatic bonds, but those are very weak.

Whenever we write chemical formulas, we always put the donor first. E.g. $"H"_2 "O"$.

> Atoms are chemically stable when they maintain charge balance and all orbital valencies are filled to capacity.

Atoms also have different radii sizes, which influence the "shape" of the bonds.

**Cations:** Donors
**Anions:** Receivers

**Covalent bonds** form when electrons are *shared* between two atoms of a molecule. Covalent bonds tend to be very strong.

If we take a look at a water molecule, the electrons in hydrogen "lean" to one side because they are shared between the hydrogen and oxygen atoms. This therefore produces a charge difference, so ice is formed through weak electrostatic bonds. That's why ice has such a low melting temperature - we're not breaking the covalent bonds, we're breaking the intermolecular electrostatic bonds.

The metals have valencies across multiple orbitals, and the electrons at the outer orbital are very loosely held (an *electron sea*). That's why they're such good conductors.

**Van der Waals bonds:** The electrostatic attraction between adjacent molecules due to an electrostatic imbalance.

Graphite has intermolecular Van der Waals bonds across sheets, which is why they are so easy to break.

The relative hardness of a mineral is controlled by its composition and bond strengths. Minerals of equal or greater hardness can abrade minerals of equal or less hardness.

The silicate tetrahedron is a complex (meaning molecule) anion with a charge of -4: ${} "Si" "O"_4^(4-) {}$. So it likes to form bonds with surrounding cations and creates long chains with other oxygen atoms from other tetrahedra.

Olivine $("Mg"_1 "Fe")_2 "Si" "O"_4$ is a **ferro-magnesium** mineral. The parentheses mean magnesium and iron can substitute for one another (you can have olivine with a lot of iron or a lot of magnesium).

## Physical properties of minerals

Lustre: The way light interacts with the mineral's surface

Metallic: Highly reflective and opaque
Vitreous: Translucent (highly transmissive) and reflective
Pearly: Opaque with an iridescent sheen
Resinous: Translucent, but not as reflective as vitreous minerals
Silky: Somewhat reflective with a fibrous texture
Dull: Not very reflective, opaque

Colour: Variable within the same mineral (e.g. rose quartz and amethyst)
Streak: Obtained by rubbing the mineral against an unglazed porcelain plate, much less variable than colour

Cleavage: Snapping cleanly in parallel planes
- Cleavage != crystal face: Cleavage will have repeating planes, crystals will instead fracture when broken
Fracture: Irregular breakage

## Covalent vs. ionic bonds

Silica tetrahedra form strong covalent bonds with each other. The rest of the valencies form weaker ionic bonds with various elements. Therefore, depending on the geometry of the tetrahedra, there will be natural planes of cleavage. When a force is applied parallel to one of these planes, the usually attracting electrostatic forces get shifted, creating a large repulsive electrostatic force that cleaves the mineral.

The higher the silica content, the greater the viscosity. Imagine a group of people running individually vs. a group of people running while holding hands - the second group is slower.
