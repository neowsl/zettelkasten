##  2026-02-02

If a point particle with charge $q_2$ moves from initial position $arrow(r)_A$ to final position $arrow(r)_B$, the change in **potential energy** is:
$$ Delta U equiv -q_2 integral_(arrow(r)_A)^(arrow(r)_B) arrow(E) dot d arrow(r) $$

If the field is formed by another **point particle** with charge $q_1$, the change in potential energy is:
$$ Delta U equiv (q_1 q_2) / (4 pi epsilon_0) [1 / arrow(r)_B - 1 / arrow(r)_A] $$

If we take $U = 0$ at $r = infinity$ then the potential energy of a pair of particles separated by a distance $r$ is
$$ U equiv (q_1 q_2) / (4 pi epsilon_0 r) $$

If the field is formed by **parallel plates** and we take $s = 0$ and $s = d$ at the negative and positive plates respectively (so $U = 0$ at the negative plate) then the potential energy of the arrangement is
$$ U equiv q_2 E s $$

## 2026-02-06

Note that equipotentials are always *perpendicular* to E-field lines.

**The potential everywhere in a conductor (in equilibrium) is the same. Always.**

A charge outside a conductor that polarises the conductor will contribute a field exactly opposite the field produced inside the conductor, creating a net zero field.

Recall:
$$ Delta V = - integral arrow(E) dot d arrow(r) $$

Thus, in 3D,
$$ arrow(E) = - (partial V) / (partial x) hat(i) - (partial V) / (partial y) hat(j) - (partial V) / (partial z) hat(k) $$

Or,
$$ arrow(E) = - gradient V $$
