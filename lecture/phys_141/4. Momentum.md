## 2025-11-05

Second of the three conserved quantities ($E$, $arrow(p)$, $arrow(L)$).

> **Momentum**
> $$ arrow(p) equiv m arrow(v) $$

Unlike $E$, $arrow(p)$ is a vector. $arrow(p)$ also cannot be transformed.

$$ K = arrow(p)^2 / (2m) $$

Unlike $E$, momentum can only change via transfers.

$$ arrow(F)_"net" = (d arrow(p)) / (d t) $$
$$ Delta arrow(p) = integral arrow(F) d t $$

## 2025-11-07

Like $E$, the momentum of a system is the sum of the momenta of its parts.

$$ arrow(p)_"sys" = sum_i arrow(p)_i = sum_i m_i arrow(v)_i $$

Collisions and explosions are two sides of the same coin.

If we can ignore other interactions during a collision,
- $arrow(p)_f = arrow(p)_i$
- $E_f = E_i$
- But $K_f$ might not necessarily $= K_i$

### 3 categories of collisions

1. **Perfectly inelastic**: Maximum irreversible conversion of $K$ to other forms.
	- $arrow(p)_f = arrow(p)_i$, but objects *stick together* and move as one.
2. **Inelastic**: Irreversible conversion of $K$ to some other form.
	- $arrow(p)_f = arrow(p)_i$
3. **Elastic**: Temporary, reversible conversion of $K$ to some other form.
	- The term *elastic* comes from the idea that you could have a spring in the middle of the collision.
	- $arrow(p)_f = arrow(p)_i$ *and* $K_f = K_i$

$$ Delta arrow(p) = -Delta arrow(p)_"ext" $$

## 2025-11-10

For elastic collisions of a stationary target:

$$ v_(1f) = (m_1 - m_2) / (m_1 + m_2) v_(1i) $$

$$ v_(2f) = (2m_1) / (m_1 + m_2) v_(1i) $$

1. $m_1 = m_2$:
	- $v_(1f) approx 0$
	- $v_(2f) approx v_(1f)$
2. $m_1 >> m_2$:
	- $v_(1f) approx v_(1i)$
	- $v_(2f) approx 2v_(1i)$
3. $m_1 << m_2$:
	- $v_(1f) approx -v_(1i)$
	- $v_(2f) approx 0$

---

Recall that $arrow(v)_(A B)$ represents the velocity of object $A$ in a reference frame in which object B is at rest (the "rest frame" of $B$).

$$ arrow(v)_(A B) equiv arrow(v)_(A C) + arrow(v)_(B C) $$

You can deduce the type of collision from the relative velocity of $A$ to $B$:
- $|arrow(v)_(A B)|^"after" < |arrow(v)_(A B)|^"before"$: inelastic
- $|arrow(v)_(A B)|^"after" = 0$: perfectly inelastic
- $|arrow(v)_(A B)|^"after" = |arrow(v)_(A B)|^"before"$: elastic

### Centre of mass

For an object of two points:
$$ x_"cm" = (m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2) / (m_1 + m_2) $$

If we choose $x = 0$ at the centre of mass (cm):
$$ x_1 / x_2 = m_2 / m_1 $$

$$ arrow(v)_"cm" = (arrow(p)_"sys") / M_"sys" $$

To an observer moving with 0 velocity relative to the centre of mass of a system, the momentum of the system is 0. If the collision is perfectly inelastic, all kinetic energy will be reduced to 0 after the collision.

## 2025-11-12

$$ arrow(v)_("cm" E) = (m_A arrow(v)_(A E) + m_B arrow(v)_(B E)) / (m_A + m_B) $$

Note that this calculation for velocity of centre of mass doesn't rely on displacement!

For a perfectly inelastic collision, in the centre of mass frame, $K_f = 0$.

$$ Delta K_("sys" E) = Delta K_("sys" "cm") $$

Change in energy ($Delta E$) is *frame-independent*. Imagine two observers at different speeds: they can both measure thermal energy with a thermometer and discover that the energy lost in a collision is the same.

---

In the **Earth frame**, a system has total kinetic energy

$$ K_("sys" E) equiv sum_i 1/2 m_i arrow(v)_(i E)^2 = 1/2 M_"sys" arrow(v)_("cm" E)^2 + sum_i 1/2 m_i arrow(v)_(i "cm")^2 $$

and total momentum

$$ arrow(p)_("sys" E) = M_"sys" arrow(v)_("cm" E) $$

In the **centre-of-mass** frame, a system has total kinetic energy

$$ K_("sys" "cm") equiv sum_i 1/2 m_i arrow(v)_(i "cm")^2 $$

and total momentum

$$ arrow(p)_("sys" E) = 0 $$

Note: For the Earth frame equation, the first part is the kinetic energy associated with the centre-of-mass motion. The second part is the kinetic energy associated with motion relative to the centre of mass. In an inelastic collision, the second part goes to 0.

Many times, it is easier to have one term for the centre-of-mass motion, and another term for the motion relative to that centre-of-mass.

*Collision always occurs*. If two particles collide in the Earth frame, they must collide in the centre-of-mass frame.

## 2025-11-14

To find the centre of mass of a *continuous* point distribution:

$$ x_"CM" = (sum_i m_i x_i) / (sum_i m_i) = (integral x d m) / (integral d m) $$

The centre of mass of a uniform rigid body (uniform thickness and density) is at its geometric centre.

---

For a rotating rigid body of a fixed axis:

$$ K = sum_i 1/2 m_i arrow(v)_i^2 = sum_i 1/2 m_i (r_i omega)^2 = sum_i 1/2 m_i r_i^2 omega^2 $$

If we define $I = sum_i m_i r_i^2$,

$$ K = 1/2 I omega^2 $$

Where $I$ is the "moment of inertia".

For combined translational ad rotational motion:

$$ K = 1/2 M_"sys" v_"CM"^2 + 1/2 I omega^2 $$
