## 2026-04-02: Propositional Logic

> A **proposition** is a well-formed sentence.

- Can be true or false

We need a way of talking about *arbitrary* ideas:
I.e. we need "atoms" of a language

Propositional variables: $p$, $q$, $r$, $s$
Constant truth values:
$T$ for true
$F$ for false

| Term             | Syntax    | Associativity |
| ---------------- | --------- | ------------- |
| Negation (not)   | $not p$   |               |
| Conjuction (and) | $p and q$ | Left-to-right |
| Disjunction (or) | $p or q$  | ^             |
| Exclusive Or     | $p xor q$ | ^             |
| Implication      | $p -> r$  | Right-to-left |
| Biconditional    | $p <-> q$ | ^             | 

(Higher on the table = higher precedence)

$p or q or v or t$ means $(((p or q) or v) or t)$
$p -> q -> r$ means $p -> (q -> r)$

---

### Implication: $p -> r$

| $p$ | $r$ | $p -> r$ |
| --- | --- | -------- |
| T   | T   | T        |
| T   | F   | F        |
| F   | T   | T        |
| F   | F   | T        |

With implication ($->$) , $p$ is called the **premise** and $r$ is called the **conclusion**.
The implication is true when $p$ and $r$ are true.
The implication is true ("vacuously") when $p$ is false.

In English, "if x, then y" is equivalent to "y if x".
"if x, then y" is also equivalent to "x only if y".

### Biconditional: $p <-> r$

| $p$ | $r$ | $p <-> r$ |
| --- | --- | --------- |
| T   | T   | T         |
| T   | F   | F         |
| F   | T   | F         |
| F   | F   | T         |

---

Steps to analyse an English sentence:
1. Assign a variable to each "atom" of the sentence
2. Translate connectives
3. Create a truth table
4. Add additional columns for intermediate logic (for compound statements)

## 2026-04-01: Circuits

Implication: $p -> r$
Contrapositive: $not r -> not p$
Converse: $r -> p$
Inverse: $not p -> not r$

Propositional logic statements with the same truth tables are considered **equivalent**.

**Canonical CNF form**: Exactly *one* representation of an expression (if they don't look the same, they aren't the same). Same applies for **canonical DNF form**.

### Canonical DNF

A.k.a. "sum of products"

1. Find all T rows in the truth table
2. For each T row, write an expression that is T in that row but no others (a **min term**)
3. Form the disjunction (OR) of the min terms

### Canonical CNF

A.k.a. "product of sums"

1. Find all F rows in the truth table
2. For each F row, write an expression that is T in every row *but* that one (a **max term**)
3. Form the conjunction (AND) of the max terms

## 2026-04-03: Equivalences

A compound proposition is a:
- **Tautology**: If it is always true
- **Contradiction**: If it is always false
- **Contingency**: If it can be either true or false

---

$A = B$ means $A$ and $B$ are the same thing written twice:
$$ p and r = p and r $$
$$ p and q and r = (p and q) and r $$
$$ p and r != r and p $$

$A equiv B$ means $A$ and $B$ have identical truth values:
$$ p and r equiv p and r $$
$$ p and r equiv r and p $$

$A <-> B$ is a **proposition** that may be true or false depending on the truth values of $A$ and $B$.
$A equiv B$ is an **assertion** over all possible truth values, that $A$ and $B$ always have the same truth values.

$A equiv B$ and $(A <-> B) equiv T$ have the same meaning.

---

### Important equivalences

- Identity
$p and T equiv p$
$p or F equiv p$
- Domination
$p or T equiv T$
$p and F equiv F$
- Idempotent
$p or p equiv p$
$p and p equiv p$
- Commutative
$p or q equiv q or p$
$p and q equiv q and p$
- Associative
$(p or q) or r equiv p or (q or r)$
$(p and q) and r equiv p and (q and r)$
- Distributive
$p and (q or r) equiv (p and q) or (p and r)$
$p or (q and r) equiv (p or q) and (p or r)$
- Absorption
$p or (p and q) equiv p$
$p and (p or q) equiv p$
- Negation
$p or not p equiv T$ (a.k.a. law of excluded middle)
$p and not p equiv F$ (a.k.a. law of non-contradiction)
- De Morgan's Laws
$not (p and r) equiv not p or not r$
$not (p or r) equiv not p and not r$
- Implication
$p -> q = not p or q$
- Contrapositive
$p -> q = not q -> not p$
- Biconditional
$p <-> q equiv (p -> q) and (q -> p)$
- Double negation
$p equiv not not p$

---

To show $A$ is *equivalent* to $B$, apply a series of logical equivalences to sub-expressions to convert $A$ to $B$.
To show $A$ is a *tautology*, apply a series of logical equivalences to sub-expressions to convert $A$ to $T$.
